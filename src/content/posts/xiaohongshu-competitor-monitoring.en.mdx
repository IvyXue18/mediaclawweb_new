> Manually tracking competitors doesn't scale. This guide walks you through building a prioritized Xiaohongshu competitor monitoring system — from rule setup to automated Lark reports — so your team catches every important move from benchmark accounts.

Anyone in content operations knows they should watch their competitors. But when it comes to **competitor monitoring**, the hardest part isn't starting — it's keeping up. Who posted? Which post is gaining traction? Is it worth responding to today? The monitoring logic is the same on Xiaohongshu and Douyin — this guide uses Xiaohongshu as the example; the only difference is whether your engagement threshold tracks likes and saves or plays and completion rate.

Most teams already have a list of benchmark accounts. The problem is that the observation process is too fragmented: search, screenshot, bookmark, log, filter, repeat — with information scattered everywhere and no reliable alert pipeline.

## Why Manual Competitor Tracking Always Falls Behind

When you're only watching a handful of accounts, scrolling through their profiles works fine. But once you're tracking ten or more, your judgment starts to drift.

You spot a competitor's post today, but it may have gone live yesterday — engagement has been climbing for hours before you even noticed. Your team knows something changed, but can't quickly tell which updates actually matter.

There's an even more common issue: too much noise. Not all benchmark accounts carry equal weight, and not every new post is worth your attention. Without a filtering mechanism, monitoring eventually becomes just another source of information overload.

What **Xiaohongshu competitor monitoring** really needs to solve is turning scattered observations into a prioritized **account tracking** system.

If you're building this for the first time, start with your core competitors. Set a conservative engagement threshold, run it for a few days, and evaluate the quality of content that gets flagged. Then gradually adjust parameters and expand coverage. Don't start by adding dozens of accounts — if your rules aren't dialed in, more data just means more noise.

### Can I Use This for My Own Accounts Too?

Yes — and you should.

Adding your own accounts to the same monitoring system doesn't just track your own metrics. It lets you compare yourself against competitors on the same scorecard.

A few practical use cases:

- **Content performance review**: Set an engagement threshold and run it over time. If your hit rate is low, your topics or presentation might need adjusting. If it suddenly spikes, it's worth digging into what you did differently during that period.
- **Posting cadence tracking**: If you manage multiple accounts or a matrix of accounts, the system automatically logs each account's posting frequency and content performance — more reliable than manual tracking, and nothing slips through the cracks.
- **Cross-comparison with competitors**: When your data and your benchmark accounts' data sit in the same Lark spreadsheet, it becomes much easier to see which topics competitors are winning on that you haven't covered, and which areas you're already outperforming them.

In short, the monitoring rules and alert workflow don't distinguish between your accounts and others. Add them in, and having all the data in one view actually gives you a more complete picture.

## FAQ

### Can I track Douyin competitor accounts the same way?

Yes. [Douyin account monitoring](/douyin/monitoring) uses the same subscription flow, filter rules, and daily Lark reports as Xiaohongshu. The difference is the threshold metric: Xiaohongshu usually sets a likes or saves threshold, Douyin leans on plays, completion rate, and follower gains. For the same competitors on both platforms, build a subscription for each and consolidate into one Feishu sheet to compare.

### Do I need a separate monitor for my own accounts?

No. The rules and reporting don't distinguish your accounts from competitors' — add your own to the same system and you can compare against competitors on one yardstick.
